Help content anyone can use

Most people read help content when something's already gone wrong. They're stressed, often on a phone, and they want one answer. I audited the broadband help centre against a reading age, rewrote the worst of it, and got the rest of the business writing accessibly too.

The audit

Before the broadband launch I went through all 38 broadband help articles. Each got a reading age, a severity rating (how bad is it if someone misunderstands this?) and a list of problems. The target was a reading age of 9 to 11, which is roughly what plain English guidance aims for and what most people can manage when they're tired or worried.

The switching article came out worst on both counts. Reading age 12 to 13, highest severity, and it's a step most people meet exactly once. It also tried to explain joining us and leaving us in the same article, so everyone had to read the half that didn't apply to them.

I split it into 2 articles, one for each direction, and rewrote both around what happens and what you need to do. That also gave the help bot a clean source for each question instead of one muddled one.

Writing for the help bot as well as people

giffgaff uses an AI help agent to answer questions before they reach a person. It can only be as good as what it reads. I wrote or reviewed 7 articles specifically so it had the information to answer common broadband questions properly: switching, contract documents, complaints, the address checker, the network and contacting the engineer.

When the rules changed, I changed the help too. For example, when we changed what people need to do for an outside install, I updated every message and help guide, shared the new wording with the advisers' team, then asked the bot the question myself to check it gave the new answer.

Error messages and contact

Earlier, working with the help and community team, I looked at which error messages pushed people to contact an adviser. Using analytics I compared how often people got in touch after seeing a particular message, before and after a rewrite. It's a useful test of an error message: did it tell people what to do, or just that something broke?

I also helped shape a plan for product teams to own their own help content, with rules for changing it so live tests and support data weren't thrown off.

Making accessibility everyone's job

Accessible writing doesn't scale if it depends on one person catching everything. So I wrote the accessibility guidelines that now sit in the company style guide: plain words, descriptive link text ("Learn more about reliable broadband", never "Learn more" on its own), meaningful alt text and inclusive language.

Then I ran a 2-part accessibility workshop open to anyone in the business. The first part was why it matters: who gets shut out when we get it wrong. The second was how to do it day to day.

The same rules are built into the content team's AI tone of voice tool, so drafts that come out of it already follow them.

Documents and vulnerable customers

Contract documents have to be available to everyone. I produced large print and braille versions of the broadband contract summary and contract information, and moved them onto a new accessible template. I also wrote the content for customers in vulnerable situations across buying and setting up broadband.

What's next

Reading age tells you a lot, but not whether people can find the right article or understand what happens next. I've planned a research day to test exactly that, with findability tasks, "what happens next" comprehension questions and highlighter tests on real articles.
